Everyday software should be a good houseguest.

It should arrive when needed, explain itself clearly, and become quiet again. We do not measure usefulness by how often an app pulls you back. We measure whether it gave back more attention than it asked for.

Five things we will not compromise on.

These principles apply to every product we choose to make.

Collect less by design

Every feature begins with a simpler question: what information can the product avoid asking for or keeping?

Longevity over lock-in

A useful tool should not become useless because an account, subscription, or network connection is absent.

Fair and honest

A genuinely useful free tier, optional subscriptions, straightforward cancellation, and no dark patterns.

Details earn trust

Copy, motion, accessibility, failure states, and support deserve the same attention as the headline feature.

A deliberate portfolio

New products begin with a durable problem, not a release calendar or a target app count.

The unglamorous work was the point.

The parts of life that no one enjoys tracking, the bills, the renewals, the little recurring chores, are exactly the things our phones should quietly help with. Instead, the tools meant to help are often noisy, packed with ads, or slowly turning into one more thing you have to manage.

We wanted the opposite. Software that does one useful thing, respects your time and your data, and then gets out of the way. So we started building it, one small app at a time, beginning with Heads-Up.

Paul & Bhatt make MundaneApps together.

We choose problems that stay useful for years, then give their ordinary details more care than they usually receive.

The renewal that quietly kept charging.

Paul once meant to cancel a work tool before its renewal date. The notice landed in a crowded inbox, the day moved on, and the reminder disappeared with it. By the time he spotted the annual charge, the refund window had closed on something he no longer used.

It was not a crisis, just an expensive little tax on forgetting. That avoidable cost stayed with him: important dates should not depend on one busy person remembering at exactly the right moment.

The call that mattered on that particular day.

Bhatt once promised to call someone close before a difficult appointment. Work filled the day, the promise slipped out of view, and he remembered the next morning. The missed call did more than disappoint; it left someone he cared about feeling alone when they had expected support.

The apology was sincere, but it could not recreate the moment. That small emotional failure made the problem feel human: remembering is often less about a task and more about showing up for another person.
